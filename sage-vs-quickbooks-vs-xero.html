Sage vs QuickBooks vs Xero: the three-way

This isn't a fresh round of testing — it's the three products people actually mean when they ask "which accounting software", pulled together from the individual trials on this site so the comparison sits in one place. Xero and QuickBooks both worked end to end. Sage's bank reconciliation still hasn't worked in any session run for this site.

The short answer

Pick Xero if: you want the fastest, lightest onboarding of the three and you're prepared to double-check its auto-suggested nominal accounts and VAT rates — it got two out of five reconciled test transactions wrong on VAT treatment.

Pick QuickBooks if: bank reconciliation is the task you care most about — it's the most capable of the three tested here, reading PDF and photo statements as well as CSV and explaining its own invoice-matching decisions in plain English. In return, expect a more conservative VAT setup: it doesn't switch VAT on by default even if you ask it to during onboarding.

Pick Sage if: its stricter compliance defaults (forced two-factor authentication, an explicit sign-off before opting out of Making Tax Digital) matter to you more than anything else here — but go in knowing that, across every session run for this site, bank reconciliation simply hasn't worked on Sage Accounting.

If you're genuinely undecided: Xero and QuickBooks can both be vouched for end to end — invoicing, bank reconciliation and a VAT return all worked on both. Sage's invoicing and VAT return worked; its bank reconciliation is still an open question, not a verdict against it, but it is a real gap in what I can tell you.

How this page was built

Rather than run a fourth round of trials, this page pulls together the genuine testing already done for two other articles on this site: Xero vs Sage (Xero's Ignite plan against Sage Accounting's Start plan, across two Sage sessions after the first one hit a platform fault) and QuickBooks vs Sage (QuickBooks' Simple Start plan, one sitting). Each of those pages has the full detail on what was actually done — invoices raised, bank statements imported, VAT returns pulled — and this page exists to put the headline findings for all three side by side rather than making you cross-reference two separate articles.

Important: all testing behind this page used sample or dummy data — a fictional "Test Company Ltd" — on genuine free trials. Nothing from my employer's systems, or any real client's, went anywhere near any of the three accounts. I also use Sage 50, the desktop product, daily at work; Sage Accounting is Sage's separate cloud product, and that distinction matters — nothing here is a verdict on Sage 50.

Because this page combines three separate sessions rather than one controlled comparison, treat it as an index into the fuller articles rather than the final word — read the linked pages for the actual walkthroughs, screenshots-in-words, and caveats behind each figure below.

The one task all three could be compared on: raising an invoice

Xero needed no template setup before the first invoice, and auto-suggested both the nominal account and the correct VAT rate from a plain-English line-item description, without being asked.

QuickBooks goes straight into the invoice editor with no separate contact-first step, and pre-fills the invoice number, date and a Net 30 due date automatically. But VAT stayed off company-wide even after choosing "Manage VAT" as an onboarding goal — the invoice saved as a flat, VAT-free amount until I went and set VAT up separately, and even then every invoice defaults to "No VAT" and needs manually switching on. The most conservative of the three on this point.

Sage does not auto-suggest a nominal account or VAT rate from the description at all — you're choosing every time, or living with an unhelpful default ("4900 – Other income"). Its invoice-address requirement is a genuine hard block, unlike Xero's soft warning, but far less clearly signposted — a small red icon rather than a popup telling you what's wrong.

Bank reconciliation: where this genuinely isn't a three-way

QuickBooks was the strongest of the three. A manually uploaded six-line CSV statement was auto-mapped correctly with zero input, it accepts PDF, JPG, PNG and HEIC statements as well as CSV, and its suggested invoice match came with a written, plain-English explanation of why the two transactions matched — something neither Xero nor Sage does. Its weak spot: a plain expense line got no auto-suggested category at all, and posting anything once VAT is switched on requires a VAT code chosen explicitly first, even on a line with no VAT amount stated.

Xero worked cleanly but was less accurate once matching was left to its own suggestions — right on nominal account and VAT rate three times out of five on a five-line test statement, wrong on a software subscription (defaulted to "Exempt Expenses" instead of the reverse charge) and on commercial rent (defaulted to 20% VAT rather than exempt).

Sage could not be tested at all, on either of two attempts. Every route into recording a bank transaction — the Banking section, the "Connect your bank" setup card, Cashbook's Money In/Money Out — hit the same platform error, reproduced from a fresh login on two separate days. This is the single biggest gap in this three-way: for the one task that matters most to daily bookkeeping, Sage Accounting simply hasn't been usable in any session run for this site.

VAT returns

All three generate the standard UK 9-box HMRC return, and all three let you view or prepare it without connecting to HMRC or filing — Xero via "VAT without MTD", QuickBooks via a legitimate "Skip set up for now" option, Sage by simply leaving the return in "Unfiled" status. None of the three testing sessions behind this page ever clicked "file" or connected to HMRC.

Xero's standout was handling the reverse charge on an overseas software subscription completely automatically, inline, with no configuration needed.

QuickBooks' return correctly reflected only what had actually been posted to the ledger — when I pointed it at a quarter with real test data, Box 1 (£90.00) and Box 4 (£7.08) tied out exactly to the one invoice and one expense I'd categorised, with the four other, still-uncategorised bank lines correctly excluded. An unreviewed bank line can't silently skew a QuickBooks VAT return.

Sage adds an explicit acknowledgement step before you can even open the return if you're opting out of Making Tax Digital — neither of the other two ask for that. Its actual return could only ever reflect the one invoice raised, though, since the bank reconciliation side never worked — so its figures aren't comparable to the other two's on a like-for-like basis.

What this page can't tell you yet

This is a synthesis of three separate sessions, not one controlled test — so treat the numbers as directional rather than perfectly matched, and read the two linked articles for the full detail behind each one. The biggest open item is Sage's bank reconciliation: until that's retested and confirmed working (or not) on a fresh account, any three-way verdict on daily bookkeeping has a genuine hole in it rather than a settled answer.

Who each one is not for

Xero isn't for you if you're planning to accept its suggested nominal accounts and VAT rates without checking them.

QuickBooks isn't for you if you want VAT switched on and correct the moment you finish onboarding — it stayed off by default in this test even after asking for it.

Sage Accounting isn't for you right now if bank reconciliation matters from day one, which is most UK small businesses — this is the one gap on this page that isn't a trade-off, it's an unresolved fault.
