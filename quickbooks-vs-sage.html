QuickBooks vs Sage: which suits a UK small business

QuickBooks's bank reconciliation is the most capable I've tested so far — it reads PDF and photo statements, not just CSV, and explains its own invoice-matching decisions in plain English. Sage Accounting's invoicing and VAT return are solid, but I still can't tell you how its bank reconciliation performs: it hit the same platform fault in this session that broke it in the Xero comparison.

Published 29 September 2026 · QuickBooks trialled for one session · Sage Accounting trialled across two sessions (see the Xero vs Sage article)

The short answer

Pick QuickBooks if: you want the most help on bank reconciliation of anything tested on this site so far — it uploads statements as PDF, JPG, PNG or HEIC as well as CSV, and its suggested invoice matches come with a written explanation rather than just a confidence tick. In exchange, its VAT is off by default even if you tell it during setup that you want to manage VAT, and it's stricter than the others about needing a VAT code chosen on every transaction once VAT is switched on.

Pick Sage if: compliance defaults matter to you more than software polish — it makes you explicitly acknowledge opting out of Making Tax Digital rather than letting you breeze past it. But I still can't vouch for its bank reconciliation: it was broken in my Xero comparison and the fault hasn't been retested here, so this page can't compare that feature between the two head to head.

If you're genuinely undecided: I can vouch for QuickBooks end to end in this test — invoicing, bank reconciliation and a VAT return all worked, and the VAT return's Box totals tied out exactly to what I'd actually posted. Sage's invoicing and VAT return worked; bank reconciliation is still an open question on Sage, not a verdict against it.

How I tested them

I signed up for a genuine free trial of QuickBooks — the Simple Start plan, 30 days, Limited Company — using a sample company called "Test Company Ltd" with no real business data anywhere near the account. In one sitting, I raised a sales invoice, set up VAT and Making Tax Digital registration details (without connecting to HMRC), created a manual bank account and imported a six-line CSV statement, matched and categorised those transactions, and pulled the standard UK VAT return for a quarter containing my test data. The Sage figures on this page are carried over from the same two testing sessions used for the Xero vs Sage article, where Sage's bank reconciliation failed with the same platform error on both attempts.

Important: all testing used sample or dummy data. Nothing from my employer's systems, or any real client's, went anywhere near either account. I also use Sage 50 — the desktop product — daily at work; Sage Accounting is Sage's separate cloud product, and I'm flagging that distinction clearly rather than letting the two blur together.

This is one sitting on QuickBooks and two on Sage (neither of them a bank-reconciliation success) — not months of daily use. Before this page is treated as final, it needs a retry of Sage's bank reconciliation on a fresh trial account to check whether the fault is fixed, and a second QuickBooks session trying something not covered here (voiding an invoice, a second VAT quarter, payroll).

Where they genuinely differ

Both do invoicing and VAT returns — that's table stakes. Bank reconciliation is where this comparison gets lopsided, for reasons explained below.

Getting set up

QuickBooks runs a long guided wizard — business name, industry (a genuinely good, searchable UK-specific list), revenue band, structure, role, income source, team size — before asking what you actually want to do in the software. I chose "Manage VAT" and "Send & track invoices" as goals partway through, which turned out to matter less than expected: see below.

Sage requires mandatory two-factor authentication before any real work can start, with no way to skip it — a genuine friction point QuickBooks didn't have in this test.

Raising an invoice

QuickBooks goes straight into the invoice editor from a "Create invoice" quick action, with no separate contact-first step. Invoice number, date and a Net 30 due date are all pre-filled. Adding a new customer or a new product/service both open full side panels rather than a quick inline add — and a new product/service requires an "Income account", effectively a mandatory nominal code, closer to Sage's model than to Xero's or FreeAgent's guess-and-go approach.

Sage does not auto-suggest a nominal account or VAT rate from the invoice description at all — the category just sits on whatever default is set in your account settings (out of the box, an unhelpful "4900 – Other income"). Sage's invoice-address requirement is a hard block, but poorly signposted: clicking Save with no address did nothing visible except a small red icon you had to notice yourself.

The real surprise was VAT, and it was on QuickBooks' side this time. Despite selecting "Manage VAT" as an onboarding goal, VAT stayed switched off company-wide — that goal only changes which dashboard tiles you see, not whether VAT is actually enabled. With VAT not yet configured, the invoice editor has no VAT column at all; the invoice saved as a flat £450.00 with no way to add tax. Fixing this needs a separate trip to VAT setup (registration number, filing frequency, scheme — I used a placeholder VAT number, never a real one, and didn't touch the Making Tax Digital/HMRC-connection step). Even after that, every individual invoice still defaults to "No VAT" via an "Invoice amounts are" dropdown, and needs switching to "Exclusive of Tax" (or Inclusive) plus a per-line VAT rate before anything is charged. Compare that with Xero and FreeAgent, both of which defaulted new invoices to Standard 20% VAT without being asked (see the FreeAgent vs Xero article) — QuickBooks is the most conservative of the three on this specific point, in a way that could genuinely under-charge VAT on a real invoice if you didn't know to check.

Bank reconciliation

QuickBooks was the strongest performer of any product tested on this site so far. No live bank was connected (deliberately, as with every other trial) — I created a manual account and uploaded a six-line CSV statement. QuickBooks auto-detected the header row, the date format, and mapped all three columns correctly with no input from me, which is a proper guided mapping step rather than a rigid expected format (compare FreeAgent's undocumented three-column, no-header requirement, covered in the FreeAgent/Xero article). It also accepts PDF, JPG, PNG and HEIC statement uploads, not just CSV/QFX/QBO/OFX/TXT — broader than any other product tested here, with a note that "our team of global experts reviews some statements to ensure accuracy", which reads as OCR or AI-assisted extraction on the back end.

The standout feature: a bank line reading "Acme Test Supplies Ltd - INV 1001" for £540.00 was immediately suggested as a match against the invoice I'd raised, and QuickBooks showed a written, plain-English rationale for the match — "The bank memo explicitly states 'Acme Test Supplies Ltd - INV 1001' along with the received amount... matches the Open Balance intended for this invoice. No service charges are mentioned, so the full amount is allocated to the invoice." No other product tested so far explains its suggested matches like this; Xero and FreeAgent just show a candidate and a confidence tick.

It isn't uniformly smarter, though. A plain expense line ("Office supplies - Staples") got no auto-suggested nominal code at all — I had to pick "Office expenses, repairs & maintenance" manually from a full chart-of-accounts-style list. The AI matching seems to apply specifically to invoice- or bill-shaped transactions with a clear reference number, not everyday expense coding. And once VAT is switched on company-wide, QuickBooks refuses to post any categorised expense without an explicit VAT code chosen first — even a plain bank line with no VAT amount stated anywhere — which is stricter than Xero, which lets you post an uncategorised-VAT expense and only warns you about it afterwards.

Sage — I still can't tell you how this performs. Every route into recording a bank transaction hit the same platform error on both of the sessions used for the Xero comparison, reproduced from a fresh login each time, across every entry point tried (Banking, the "Connect your bank" setup card, and Cashbook's Money In/Money Out). I haven't retried it for this article, so this isn't a fresh result — it's the same known gap, carried over and flagged rather than quietly dropped.

VAT return

QuickBooks gates its entire VAT area behind a "Set up Making Tax Digital for VAT" screen that wants either full HMRC/Government Gateway authorisation, or a declaration that you're exempt or using other software — I did neither, and used the legitimate "Skip set up for now" option instead, which let me into the return without lying about MTD status or connecting to HMRC. The actual return is the standard 9-box UK form, fully viewable and editable locally, with no "File online" or "Mark as filed" action ever used. Once I pointed it at the quarter containing my test data, every box tied out exactly to what I'd actually posted: £90.00 VAT due on the £450.00 invoice (Box 1), and — critically — only £7.08 reclaimed on purchases (Box 4), because that's the only transaction I'd actually categorised and posted with a VAT code; the other bank lines still sitting in "Pending" correctly didn't feed into the figures at all. That's a genuinely reassuring result: an unreviewed bank line can't silently skew a QuickBooks VAT return.

Sage's VAT return used the same standard 9-box HMRC layout, and added one thing neither Xero nor QuickBooks had: opting out of Making Tax Digital requires ticking an explicit acknowledgement ("I acknowledge that my business is not required to register for Making Tax Digital") before you can even open the return screen. I can't compare the actual VAT figures fairly between the two, though — because Sage's bank reconciliation never worked, its return only ever reflected the one invoice raised, with nothing to reclaim, rather than a full period of both sales and purchases the way the QuickBooks figure does.

What annoyed me about each

QuickBooks: VAT staying off by default even after telling the setup wizard you want to manage VAT is a genuine trap — it's easy to imagine someone sending out several invoices before noticing none of them charge VAT. The mandatory VAT-code-before-posting rule on bank transactions, once VAT is on, is also more friction than Xero for quickly clearing a backlog of expenses.

Sage: the bank reconciliation fault is the obvious one, still unresolved as far as this site can confirm. Beyond that: no auto-suggestion at all from invoice descriptions means more manual choosing on every single invoice, and mandatory two-factor authentication with no skip option is real friction before you've done anything.

Switching between them

I haven't migrated a chart of accounts, historical invoices, or VAT history between the two, and I'm not going to guess at how painful that is. If you're considering a switch, check each vendor's own migration documentation or ask their support before committing — not something either of us should take on faith from a comparison page.

Who this is not for

QuickBooks isn't for you if you want VAT switched on and charging correctly the moment you finish onboarding — in this test it stayed off by default even after I said I wanted to manage VAT, and needed a deliberate second trip to fix. It suits someone happy to double-check VAT status explicitly before sending a first real invoice, in exchange for genuinely the best bank reconciliation experience tested on this site so far.

Sage Accounting isn't for you right now if bank reconciliation matters from day one — which, realistically, is almost every UK small business — because I still haven't been able to confirm it works. Worth repeating: this is Sage's cloud product, not Sage 50, the desktop software I actually use day to day at work — the two are genuinely different products, and nothing here should be read as a verdict on Sage 50.
